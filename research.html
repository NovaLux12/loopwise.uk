Open access, or no access

Medtronic already publishes most of this. Nobody wrote it down.

There is no public API documentation for CareLink. There is no developer programme. And yet most of what a third-party client needs is already served without a login, undocumented, in production. So the work nobody has to do, everybody does twice.

The state of it

One vendor has an API. Four do not.

As of October 2026, this is the documented position across the major diabetes device manufacturers.

Dexcom genuinely does this well. A UK user with a compatible G6 or G7 can grant a third-party application access today, through OAuth, without asking anyone’s permission. That is the exception, and it proves the pattern is workable rather than impossible.

Two things worth knowing. Dexcom ONE does not support Dexcom Share at all, so the standard workaround fails for roughly half of the UK Dexcom installed base. And Tandem is the only vendor that had undocumented endpoints and then deliberately removed them during a platform migration.

What you are entitled to

Rights that stop at the account record

UK GDPR Article 15 gives you the right to a copy of the personal data a company already holds about you. That data includes what your devices observed, held in your account.

Article 20 covers portability of data you have provided. The ICO reads that as including data observed from using a device. It does not cover inferred or derived data, and it does not oblige a company to hold data in real time in order to hand it over.

What none of this does is require a manufacturer to build a product that makes data accessible in the first place. A right to a copy of what is collected is not a right to continuous access to what is generated. Those are different things, and only one of them exists.

Elsewhere

The direction regulators are taking

The EU
The EU Data Act covers connected medical devices explicitly. From 12 September 2025, users in the EU can access data their devices generate and pass it to third parties. It does not apply in the UK.
The US
FDA cybersecurity guidance for medical devices, 2023, states that security controls on a device should not be intended to stop a user accessing their own device data.
The UK
Nothing. No parliamentary debate, no APPG report, no CMA action, no ICO decision, no MHRA position on patient access to raw device data. As of October 2026 the question has been left to the device community and to regulatory inertia.
A finding

A European regulator has already looked at this

In 2024, Italy’s data protection authority, Garante, fined Medtronic €300,000 over CareLink transparency.

That is worth more than any opinion I could offer. A regulator with enforcement powers looked at this specific system and took action. I am not asserting that anyone chose to withhold access. I am pointing at a finding that was made without me having to argue anything.

What people do about it

Everyone builds it themselves

There is no supported route, so the ecosystem reverse engineered its own. Open-source clients exist for CareLink, for Tandem’s portal, for Abbott’s Libre data. They work. They are maintained by people who needed them. They break when a vendor re-platforms, because they depend on undocumented behaviour the vendor is under no obligation to preserve.

That is the actual cost. Not the reverse engineering. The fragility.

And the safety argument is fair. Connected devices can be attacked. The FDA recalled Medtronic MiniMed pumps in 2019 for an exploitable wireless vulnerability, and the same year issued a safety communication advising against unauthorised diabetes devices after an insulin overdose. Manufacturers operate in a liability regime where opening a data pathway genuinely adds risk. “They should just publish an API” skips over that.

The work

I did not have to log in to find most of this

The research repository is a read-only map of CareLink’s API surface. Every request was unauthenticated. No token, no cookie, no vendor API key, no login, no credentials used at all. Around 600 requests across several days, rate-limited to one per second, with a hard per-pass cap recorded in the logs and never exceeded.

The findingNo. 001
The short version

The gap is not secrecy. The gap is documentation.

One unauthenticated file names every device family Medtronic manufactures. Another answers unauthenticated for 117 countries. A third lets you establish which API routes exist without ever holding a token. A configuration endpoint returns 69 keys with no credential required. It has been there the whole time.

Anyone building a client for CareLink reverse engineers the same surface independently, because nothing tells them the half of it that is already published. That work is duplicated across every project in this space, and none of it was necessary.

Three things it took a while to pin down

  • The app-version parameter looks like a dial with an open range. It is not. It resolves to exactly one of two documents. Probing a new version number usually re-derives something already known, which closes off an entire class of wasted work for anyone who knows it.
  • Several routes are POST-only and invisible to a GET-only probe. A route answering method not allowed is a live route, not a dead one.
  • Booleans in the public configuration arrive as the strings "true" and "false". A strict equality check against false therefore never fires and fails silently. I found this because a check I trusted was returning nothing.

What I did not find

No exploitable vulnerability. No patient data is reachable without a valid token. Redirect URI validation is strict. TLS is enforced correctly. The one credential appearing in a public JavaScript bundle is verified dead. What exists is a ranked list of hardening gaps, none of them exploitable as far as I can tell.

One finding I am not publishing. There is a pair of API keys served unauthenticated on the US and trials hosts. That is a real issue and it is not mine to publish. It is documented in the repository as withheld, to be disclosed properly rather than dropped on a blog.

Where I get to

I maintain Loopwise alone, in the UK, in my own time. No company funds it, no patient pays for it, and nothing on this page or in that repository is monetised. That is a deliberate choice, not modesty: the moment this becomes a business, the argument stops being about patient data access and starts being about my revenue.
